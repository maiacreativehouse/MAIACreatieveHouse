Part 1: The Idea

Pick one idea

The single most common mistake in a first carousel is trying to fit an entire strategy deck into eight slides. A carousel isn't a summary of everything you know. It's one idea, walked through slowly enough that someone actually absorbs it.

Before you open any design tool, finish this sentence out loud: "By the last slide, I want the reader to understand ___." If you can't finish it in one clause, the idea isn't ready yet.

A good test: could this carousel be a single sentence someone repeats to a colleague afterward? If yes, you have your idea. If it takes a paragraph to explain, it's still two or three ideas fighting for one carousel.
Your one sentence

Type it here, then copy it somewhere you'll keep. It's the sentence you check every slide against.

Part 2: Structure

The slide map

Every carousel that works follows some version of the same shape: a hook that earns the swipe, a short build that adds context, a middle that delivers the actual substance, and a close that gives the reader somewhere to go. Here's the map for a standard 7-slide carousel.

01
Hook Slide
The one line that earns the swipe
A claim, a number, or a contrast. No logo-heavy branding here, no long sentence. See Part 2b for the formula.
02
Context Slide
Why this is true, or why it matters now
One or two sentences. This is where you earn the reader's trust that the rest is worth reading.
03-05
Substance Slides
The actual steps, reasons, or examples
One idea per slide. If a slide needs two headlines, it's two slides. Number them if the sequence matters.
06
Recap Slide
The one sentence from Part 1, restated
Say the whole point again, in one line. This is what people screenshot.
07
Close Slide
Where the reader goes next
A question, a follow prompt, or an offer. Never more than one ask.
Eight or nine slides is fine if the idea genuinely needs the room. Twelve is almost never earned. If you're past ten, you likely have two carousels, not one.
Part 2b: The Hook

Writing the hook slide

The hook slide is doing the same job as the first line of a post: it's the whole reason someone taps to slide two. Most hook slides fail because they describe the topic instead of making a claim about it.

"[Surprising number or contrast] + [why that's worth your next ten seconds]"
Example: "Carousels get 11x more interactions than static images. We still post 6x more images."
"[Common belief], but [what's actually true]"
Example: "More slides doesn't mean more value. Most carousels should be shorter than they are."
"[A specific moment or decision] that changed [outcome]"
Example: "I killed a client's tagline three days before launch. Here's why."
The hook slide has one job: earn the second slide. It does not need to explain the whole idea. That's what the rest of the carousel is for.
Part 2c: The Words

Writing the slide copy

This is where most non-designers actually get stuck. Not on fonts, on the blank slide. You have the map and the hook, and now each slide needs words, and the instinct is to write a paragraph. Don't. A slide is read in about two seconds, on a phone, by someone half-scrolling.

  • Headline under ten words. If it needs a comma, it's probably two headlines.
  • Body under 25 words, and only if the headline can't stand alone. Many slides don't need one.
  • One thought per slide. If you write "also" or "and another thing," that's your next slide.
  • Write every slide as plain text first, in a doc, before you touch a design tool. Read them top to bottom. If the sequence makes sense as a list, it will make sense as a carousel.
Read your slides out loud in order. Anywhere you pause to explain something, the slide is missing a line. Anywhere you skim, the slide has too many.

Now the idea is mapped.
Time to make it look like something.

Part 3: Design

Design without a designer

You don't need original illustration or a brand system to make a carousel look intentional. You need four things held consistent across every slide.

RuleWhy it matters
One font pairingA serif for headlines, a sans for body, used the same way on every slide. Switching fonts mid-carousel reads as unfinished.
One color systemBackground, text, and one accent color. Pick these once, lock them, don't second-guess slide to slide.
Consistent marginsSame padding around text on every slide. Inconsistent margins are the fastest way to look amateur, even with good content.
A repeating anchor elementA small logo mark, a page number, or a thin rule in the same spot on every slide. It signals the set belongs together before anyone reads a word.
Resist the urge to make each slide "more designed" than the last. The best carousels look almost plain, so the ideas do the work instead of the graphics.
Part 3b: Tools

Tools & templates

Any of these will get you there. The tool matters less than sticking to the four rules above.

  • Canva — fastest to start, has native multi-page carousel templates, easiest for a first attempt.
  • Figma — better if you want a reusable template you'll adjust weekly, more control over exact spacing.
  • Google Slides / Keynote — genuinely fine for a first carousel. Export each slide as an image. Nobody can tell.
Whichever tool you use, build one template slide first with your fonts, colors, and margins locked in, then duplicate it for every slide after. Never design each slide from a blank canvas.

Part 4: Publishing

The caption that carries it

The caption isn't a summary of the carousel. It's the reason someone opens the carousel in the first place, and the place where you add what didn't fit on the slides.

A caption has three jobs

1. Restate the hook, in different words than slide one.
2. Add one layer the slides didn't have room for, a source, a caveat, a personal note.
3. Give a real next step, a question or an offer, not "swipe for more" after they've already swiped.

Part 4b: Platform Notes

LinkedIn specifics

Everything above applies to any platform. These are the details that are LinkedIn-only, worth checking before you hit publish.

WhatSpec
File typePDF (LinkedIn's native "document post" format), not a video or image carousel
Dimensions1080 x 1350px per page (4:5), portrait reads best on mobile feed
Page count7-10 pages is the sweet spot; LinkedIn allows up to 300 but nobody should use that many
UploadExport your design as a single multi-page PDF, then use "Add a document" when composing the post
LinkedIn's document viewer auto-plays the next page if someone doesn't tap, but dwell time on early pages is still what earns algorithmic reach. A slow, clear first three slides matter more than a clever last one.
Put your real CTA in the caption, not just the last slide. A large share of readers never make it to slide seven, but almost everyone reads the caption first.
